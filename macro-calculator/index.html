How to Use the Macro Calculator

  1. Enter a daily calorie target — the TDEE calculator gives you one.
  2. Enter your bodyweight, used to express protein per kilogram.
  3. Pick a split. Balanced works for most people; high protein helps during a deficit.
  4. Read the grams — protein and carbs are 4 cal/g, fat is 9 cal/g.

How It's Calculated

Macros are a percentage of calories converted to grams using the energy density of each nutrient.

Protein g = calories × % ÷ 4
Carbs g   = calories × % ÷ 4
Fat g     = calories × % ÷ 9

Worked example: 2,500 calories on a balanced 30/40/30 split gives 750 calories of protein (188 g), 1,000 of carbs (250 g) and 750 of fat (83 g).

Protein is the macro worth getting right. Research broadly supports 1.6 to 2.2 g per kg of bodyweight for people training with weights, with the upper end more useful in a calorie deficit, where protein protects lean mass. Below about 1.2 g/kg, active people tend to lose muscle alongside fat.

Fat has a floor too — roughly 0.5 g per kg — because it is required for hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption. Very low fat intakes sustained for long periods are a bad trade. Carbohydrate is what remains, and it is largely a performance and preference decision rather than a health one.

Fiber is set at roughly 14 g per 1,000 calories, which is the standard dietary recommendation and is missed by most people by a wide margin.

Frequently Asked Questions

What macro split is best?

For most goals, the split matters far less than total calories and adequate protein. Hit your calorie target and your protein target, and the carb-to-fat ratio underneath becomes largely a question of what keeps you full, fuels your training and fits your life. Adherence beats optimisation every time.

Do I need high protein to lose fat?

Higher protein helps meaningfully during a deficit — it preserves lean mass, is more satiating per calorie, and has a slightly higher thermic effect. Around 2 g per kg of bodyweight is a reasonable target for people training in a deficit. Beyond roughly 2.4 g/kg the added benefit is minimal.

Do carbs at night cause fat gain?

No. Total energy balance over days and weeks determines fat gain, not the clock. Meal timing has modest effects on training performance and sleep quality for some people, but the idea that carbohydrate eaten after a certain hour is stored differently is not supported.
