How Many Bundles of Shingles Do I Need for a Roof?

3 August 2026 · 4 min read

Roofing is counted in squares, sold in bundles, and calculated from an area larger than the house it sits on. Each of those three facts trips people up, so take them in order.

What a roofing square is

A square is 100 square feet of finished roof surface. It is the unit every roofer, supplier and estimate uses, and it exists because 100 square feet of shingles is a convenient quantity to price and ship.

Three bundles cover one square for standard three-tab and architectural shingles. Heavier designer and premium shingles run four or five bundles per square — the coverage is printed on the wrapper, and it is worth reading before you multiply.

Pitch changes everything

Your roof covers more surface than the footprint of the building beneath it, because it slopes. The pitch multiplier converts one to the other — it is the hypotenuse of a triangle with a rise equal to your pitch over a run of 12.

Multiplier = √(rise² + 12²) ÷ 12

To find your pitch without climbing anywhere: in the attic, hold a level against a rafter, mark 12 inches along it, and measure straight up from that mark to the rafter. That vertical measurement is your rise. Most homes fall between 4/12 and 8/12.

Waste for hips and valleys

Every hip and valley means cutting shingles at an angle, and angled offcuts are rarely reusable.

  • 10% — a simple gable roof with two planes.
  • 15% — hips, valleys, dormers.
  • 20% — complex roofs with many planes and penetrations.

The worked example

40 × 30 ft building, 6/12 pitch, 15% waste

Footprint: 1,200 sq ft

Roof area: 1,200 × 1.118 = 1,342 sq ft

Squares: 13.42

With waste: 1,543 sq ft

47 bundles

Measure the roof, not the ground

The footprint method above assumes a simple gable and no overhangs. Real roofs have eaves projecting a foot or two on every side, and that extra area is shingled like everything else — add it to the footprint before applying the multiplier, or you will finish about a square short.

For a roof with several planes, the reliable method is to measure each plane individually from the ground, plane by plane, and add the areas. It takes longer than one multiplication but it is the only approach that survives dormers and additions.

Tear-off or overlay

Most building codes permit two layers of asphalt shingles on a roof. Laying a second layer over the first is cheaper and faster, and it is almost always the wrong call: you cannot inspect the decking underneath, the new shingles sit on an uneven surface, the added weight is real, and the eventual tear-off costs more.

A tear-off adds disposal — a roof generates several tons of debris — but it is the only way to find soft decking, and soft decking found after the shingles are on is a much more expensive discovery.

What the bundle count leaves out

Field shingles are not the whole order. Three things are bought separately and are routinely forgotten:

  • Starter strip along every eave and rake — roughly 100 linear feet per bundle. Using cut-up field shingles instead is a common shortcut that voids many warranties.
  • Ridge cap along every ridge and hip — 20 to 35 linear feet per bundle.
  • Underlayment — #15 felt covers about 400 sq ft per roll, synthetic around 1,000. Ice and water shield at the eaves is separate again, at roughly 200 sq ft per roll, and is required by code in cold climates.

Nails run about 2.5 lb per square. Buy from a single lot number where you can: colour varies between production runs, and a patch from a second lot shows for the life of the roof.
