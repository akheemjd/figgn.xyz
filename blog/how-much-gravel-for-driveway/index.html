How Much Gravel Do I Need for a Driveway?

3 August 2026 · 5 min read

Gravel is sold by the cubic yard or by the ton, delivered by a truck that will not make a second trip for free. Ordering short means a bare patch and another delivery fee; ordering long means a pile you will be raking into the flowerbeds for a year. Getting it right takes five minutes and a tape measure.

Step 1: Measure the driveway

Length and width in feet, measured along the ground. A single-car driveway is usually 10 to 12 feet wide, a double 20 to 24. Add the parking apron and any turnaround as separate rectangles rather than trying to average an odd shape — measure each piece, work it out, and add the results together.

Multiply length by width for your square footage. A 40 by 12 foot driveway is 480 square feet.

Step 2: Decide how deep

This is where most estimates go wrong, because the honest answer depends on what is already there.

  • Topping up an existing gravel drive: 2 to 3 inches of new stone over the old surface.
  • A new driveway on bare soil: 8 to 12 inches total, built in layers, not one deep pour of a single stone size.
  • Heavy vehicles, trailers, RVs: add 2 inches to whatever you had planned.

A new driveway is three layers. The base is 4 to 6 inches of large angular stone — #3 or crusher run — compacted hard. The middle is 3 to 4 inches of #57 or similar. The top is 2 to 3 inches of the finish stone you actually want to look at and drive on. Each layer gets compacted before the next goes down, and each one needs its own calculation.

Step 3: Convert to cubic yards

Suppliers do not sell square feet. Depth in inches gets divided by 12 to become feet, and cubic feet get divided by 27 to become cubic yards, because a cubic yard is a cube three feet on each side.

Cubic yards = (length × width × (depth ÷ 12)) ÷ 27
Tons = cubic yards × density

Density is where gravel differs from most materials. Crushed stone runs about 1.4 tons per cubic yard, pea gravel 1.3, road base 1.5, river rock 1.2. If your supplier quotes by the ton and you calculated yards, this is the number that connects them — and the gravel calculator does both conversions at once.

The worked example

40 × 12 ft driveway, 4 inch top layer, crushed stone

Area: 40 × 12 = 480 sq ft

Volume: 480 × (4 ÷ 12) = 160 cubic feet

Cubic yards: 160 ÷ 27 = 5.93

Tons: 5.93 × 1.4 = 8.3

5.93 yd³  /  8.3 tons

That is one layer. The same driveway built new from bare soil, with 5 inches of base and 3 inches of middle underneath that top layer, needs roughly 17.8 cubic yards in total — three separate orders of three different stone sizes, or one delivery of each.

Add for compaction

Angular crushed stone and road base lose 10 to 20 percent of their loose volume once plated or rolled. If the finished depth matters — and on a driveway it does — order about 15 percent more than the calculated figure. Rounded stone like pea gravel and river rock is not compacted and does not need the allowance, beyond a little extra for low spots.

What it costs

Bulk gravel typically runs $30 to $60 per cubic yard for common crushed stone, delivered, with decorative river rock considerably higher. Delivery is usually a flat fee and many suppliers set a minimum load. Prices swing hard by region and by how far the truck has to drive, so treat any figure you read online as a starting point and get a local quote before budgeting.

Before the truck arrives

  • Mark exactly where you want it dumped. A driver will not reposition ten tons.
  • Check overhead clearance for the truck bed — branches and power lines.
  • Have the base graded and compacted, with a slight crown so water runs off.
  • Lay geotextile fabric under a new driveway. It stops the stone migrating into the soil, which is what turns a good driveway into a muddy one after two winters.
  • Line up help. Spreading 8 tons by wheelbarrow is a long day; a skid steer is half an hour.

Run your own numbers before you call the supplier — knowing both the yards and the tons means you can compare quotes whichever unit they price in.
