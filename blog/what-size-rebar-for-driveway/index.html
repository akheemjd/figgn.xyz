What Size Rebar Do I Need for a Concrete Driveway?

3 August 2026 · 4 min read

Rebar does not stop concrete cracking. Concrete cracks — that is what it does. What steel does is hold the crack closed so the two sides stay level and keep carrying load. Get the size, the spacing and the placement right and a crack is invisible. Get them wrong and it becomes a step.

Bar size: #3, #4 or #5

The number is the diameter in eighths of an inch, so #4 is 4/8 — half an inch.

  • #3 (3/8") — patios, sidewalks, light slabs under 4 inches.
  • #4 (1/2") — the standard for residential driveways and garage slabs. This is the answer for most people reading this.
  • #5 (5/8") — footings, thick slabs, heavy vehicles, poor soil.

Wire mesh is an alternative for light-duty flatwork, but it gets walked flat into the subgrade during a pour and ends up doing nothing. Rebar on chairs stays where you put it.

Spacing

Spacing is measured on centre, in both directions, forming a grid:

  • 12 inches — heavy loads, thick slabs, weak subgrade.
  • 18 inches — the standard residential driveway grid.
  • 24 inches — light-duty slabs only.

Tighter spacing distributes load better but costs more steel and more tying time. Local code or an engineer overrides any rule of thumb, and for anything structural, that is who decides.

Counting the bars

The count is not span divided by spacing. There is a bar at both ends of the run as well as at every interval between them, so you always add one — and you start from the clear span after subtracting edge cover, which is 3 inches where concrete is cast against earth.

Bars = ⌊ (clear span ÷ spacing) ⌋ + 1

Overlap where bars meet

Rebar comes in 20 ft sticks, so any run longer than that needs a splice. Overlap the two bars by about 40 bar diameters — 20 inches for #4, 25 inches for #5 — and tie the lap in at least two places. Both lengths count toward your order, which is the step most estimates skip.

The worked example

20 × 40 ft driveway, #4 at 18 inches, 3 inch cover

Clear area: 19.5 × 39.5 ft

Bars lengthwise: (19.5 ÷ 1.5) + 1 = 14 bars at 39.5 ft

Bars crosswise: (39.5 ÷ 1.5) + 1 = 27 bars at 19.5 ft

Linear feet: 553 + 527 = 1,080 ft, plus laps

~57 sticks of 20 ft

That grid has 378 intersections to tie. Bring more tie wire than you think, and a twisting tool — doing 378 by hand with pliers is how a Saturday disappears.

Control joints still matter

Steel does not remove the need for control joints. Concrete shrinks as it cures and will crack somewhere — joints decide where. Cut or tool them at a depth of about a quarter of the slab thickness, in panels no larger than roughly 10 to 12 feet for a 4-inch slab, keeping panels close to square rather than long and thin.

Saw-cut joints go in within 6 to 24 hours of the pour. Wait longer and the slab has already chosen its own crack pattern for you.

Placement is half the job

Steel belongs in the middle third of the slab depth, not on the ground. Rebar lying on the subgrade does nothing structurally and rusts from below. Use chairs or concrete dobies every 3 to 4 feet to hold the grid at height, and keep bars 3 inches back from any edge cast against earth, 2 inches from formed edges exposed to weather.

Do not pull the grid up as you pour. It is a popular shortcut and it leaves the steel at a random, unknowable depth — which is the same as not knowing whether it is doing anything at all.
