How Much Does It Cost to Build a Deck?

3 August 2026 · 5 min read

A deck is two projects stacked on each other. Underneath is a structure that has to satisfy a building inspector; on top is a surface that has to satisfy you. They are priced very differently, and confusing them is why deck quotes feel arbitrary.

The per-square-foot benchmark

Installed in 2026, decks run roughly $25 to $60 per square foot in pressure-treated wood and $45 to $85 in composite. On a standard 320 sq ft (16 × 20) deck that is about $6,000 to $15,500 for wood and $13,000 to $24,000 for composite.

Materials alone are far lower: pressure-treated decking boards run about $3 to $6 per square foot, composite $5 to $14. The gap between those numbers and the installed price is labour, which is commonly 50 to 60% of a deck job.

The frame is the same either way

This is the detail that reframes the wood-versus-composite decision: the substructure is pressure-treated lumber regardless of what you walk on. Joists, beams and posts do not change. The entire price difference lives in the decking boards and the railing.

Which means composite is a smaller premium than the headline numbers suggest, and that re-decking an existing sound frame later is far cheaper than a new build.

Joist spacing and beams

16 inches on centre handles most decking laid perpendicular to the joists. Diagonal layouts need 12 inches, because the diagonal span between joists is longer than the straight one. Most composite manufacturers specify 16 inches perpendicular and 12 diagonal — installing outside their spec can void the warranty, which is an expensive way to save on lumber.

Beam size follows span and joist length, and this is where a span table or an engineer earns their keep. Doubled 2×10 is common for residential spans; going bigger to be safe is cheap insurance compared to a sagging deck.

Footings and permits

Footings must reach below the frost line, which varies from a foot in the south to four feet or more in northern states. Each footing takes concrete — a 12-inch tube 3 feet deep is about 2.4 cubic feet, or four to five 60 lb bags.

Decks nearly always need a permit and an inspection, and the inspector cares about the structure, not the surface. Budget a few hundred dollars and some calendar time. Building without one surfaces later, usually at the worst moment: when you sell the house.

The worked example

16 × 12 ft deck, 16" joists, 5/4×6 boards

Area: 192 sq ft

26 board rows · 29 boards of 16 ft

13 joists at 12 ft

DIY materials, pressure-treated: roughly $2,500–$4,000

Installed: roughly $5,000–$11,500

Freestanding costs less than attached

A deck that does not touch the house avoids the ledger connection entirely, along with its flashing, its bolts and the inspection scrutiny that comes with it. It needs an extra beam line and a few more footings, which is usually cheaper than doing the attachment properly.

For a ground-level deck it is often the better build as well as the cheaper one, since nothing is penetrating your home's weather envelope.

The ledger board is the part that fails

If your deck attaches to the house, it hangs from a ledger board bolted to the rim joist. That connection is the single most common point of catastrophic deck failure, and almost every collapse traces back to it — nailed instead of bolted, or flashed badly so water rotted the rim joist behind it.

It needs structural bolts or approved screws in a specified pattern, and proper flashing above and behind. It is not the place to save $60, and it is the first thing an inspector will look at.

What pushes the number up

  • Height. A deck 30 inches or more above grade needs railings by code, and railing is expensive per linear foot.
  • Stairs. A stair run commonly adds $500 to $2,000, including its landing pad.
  • Railing upgrades. Aluminium, cable and glass are the largest hidden swing in any deck quote — often more than the decking tier itself.
  • Demolition. Removing an old deck adds a few dollars per square foot.
  • Site conditions. Slopes, rock and poor access all add labour hours.

One thing worth knowing before you choose: national remodelling data consistently shows wood decks recouping a slightly higher share of their cost at resale than composite ones, largely because they cost less to build in the first place. Composite wins on maintenance, not on return.
