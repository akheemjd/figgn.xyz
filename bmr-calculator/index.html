BMR is what you'd burn lying still for 24 hours — breathing, pumping blood, maintaining temperature. If you sat completely still watching television you'd burn a few percent more. It's not a calorie target; nobody spends the day in a coma.

These are population-average estimates, not medical advice. Individual metabolism, body composition and health conditions vary widely — talk to a doctor or dietitian before making significant changes.

How to Use the BMR Calculator

  1. Pick imperial or metric — the inputs change to match.
  2. Enter weight, height and age — all three shift the result meaningfully.
  3. Select sex at birth; the equation uses different constants for each.
  4. For a figure that includes daily activity, move on to the TDEE calculator.

How It's Calculated

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, the standard since 1990. It outperforms the older Harris-Benedict on modern populations.

Male:   BMR = 10w + 6.25h − 5a + 5
Female: BMR = 10w + 6.25h − 5a − 161
w = weight in kg, h = height in cm, a = age in years

Worked example: a 30-year-old man, 82 kg and 178 cm: (10 × 82) + (6.25 × 178) − (5 × 30) + 5 = 820 + 1,112.5 − 150 + 5 = 1,788 cal/day at complete rest.

Roughly 60 to 75% of most people's total daily burn is BMR. It falls with age mostly because lean mass falls with age, which is the argument for resistance training that has nothing to do with how you look.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between BMR and TDEE?

BMR is what you burn at complete rest. TDEE is BMR multiplied by an activity factor, and includes walking, working, training, and even digesting food. If you're planning meals, TDEE is the number that matters; BMR is the floor underneath it.

Which BMR formula is most accurate?

Mifflin-St Jeor, for most people. It replaced Harris-Benedict because it performs better on contemporary populations. Katch-McArdle can be more accurate still, but it requires a body fat percentage measured with reasonable precision, which most people don't have.

Can I increase my BMR?

Somewhat, and mostly by building lean mass through resistance training. Muscle is metabolically active, so more of it raises the floor. The effect is modest — tens of calories per pound of muscle, not hundreds. Claims about foods or supplements raising BMR don't hold up.
