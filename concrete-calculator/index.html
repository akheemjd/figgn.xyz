How to Use the Concrete Calculator

  1. Measure the length and width of the pour in feet. Split L-shaped slabs into rectangles and run each one.
  2. Set the depth — 4" for a standard slab, 12" for a footing, 24" for a post hole, or type a custom depth.
  3. Pick your bag size and waste factor. 10% is standard and covers spillage, an uneven subgrade and over-dug holes.
  4. Use cubic yards when booking ready-mix, and the bag count when buying at a store.

How It's Calculated

Volume is area times depth. Depth is in inches, so it's divided by 12 to get feet; cubic feet are divided by 27 because a cubic yard is 3 × 3 × 3 feet.

Cubic feet = length × width × (depth ÷ 12)
Cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27
Bags = ⌈ cubic feet × (1 + waste ÷ 100) ÷ bag yield ⌉

Worked example: a 20 ft × 10 ft slab at 4 inches is 200 sq ft × 0.333 ft = 66.7 cubic feet, or 2.47 yd³ of finished slab. With the standard 10% waste factor you order 2.72 yd³ — 73.3 cubic feet — which is 163 sixty-pound bags or 123 eighty-pound bags. Without any waste allowance the bare figures are 149 and 112 — worth knowing, but not worth ordering to.

Bag yields are the printed volumes: a 60 lb bag makes about 0.45 cubic feet of mixed concrete and an 80 lb bag about 0.6. That means 60 bags of 60 lb, or 45 bags of 80 lb, per cubic yard — the number that decides bags versus truck.

Round post holes aren't rectangles. For a round hole, area is π × radius², so a 12 inch diameter hole is 0.785 sq ft. Enter that as your length and width equivalent, or measure the hole as a square of the same area.

Bags or a Truck?

Volume decides this, not budget. Both ends of the range have a clear winner.

VolumeMethodWhy
Under 0.5 yd³Bags, hand mixedUnder 25 bags. No delivery minimum, no short-load fee, no waste.
0.5–1 yd³Bags with a mixerDoable but real work. Rent a mixer and bring help.
1–2 yd³Either — trailer mixer or ready-mixThe crossover. A short-load fee may still beat 90 bags.
Over 2 yd³Ready-mixConsistent mix, one continuous pour, no fighting the set time.

The hidden constraint is time, not money. Concrete starts setting as soon as it's wet, and a slab poured in batches over three hours has cold joints through it — weak lines where one batch had already stiffened before the next arrived. A truck delivers the whole pour at once, which is why the crossover point is lower than the raw cost comparison suggests.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I order ready-mix or mix it myself?

Volume decides. Under about half a cubic yard, bags are simpler and cheaper. Between half a yard and two yards, bags still work if you have a mixer and help — but it's hard physical work against a clock. Above two yards, order ready-mix. A cubic yard is 45 eighty-pound bags or 60 sixty-pound bags, and mixing that by hand while keeping the mix consistent and beating the set time isn't realistic for most people.

How much does concrete cost?

Ready-mix delivered typically runs $140–200 per cubic yard, plus delivery and a short-load fee of roughly $60–150 on orders under four or five yards. Bagged works out considerably more per yard: at $5–8 for an 80 lb bag, the 45 bags in a cubic yard land near $225–360. Prices swing hard by region and year — treat these as a starting point and get a local quote before budgeting.

Do I need rebar in a concrete slab?

For anything carrying vehicle weight, yes. A residential driveway or garage slab normally uses #3 or #4 rebar on an 18–24" grid, or welded wire mesh for lighter duty. Footings almost always require rebar and are usually governed by local code, so check before you pour. A small patio or shed pad on well-compacted base can go without steel, though fibre reinforcement helps control cracking. Worth understanding: reinforcement doesn't stop concrete cracking — it holds the crack together. Control joints are what manage where the cracks go.
