How to Use the BMI Calculator

  1. Pick imperial or metric — the inputs change to match.
  2. Enter your weight and height. Height is squared in the formula, so small errors matter.
  3. Read the BMI and category, with the scale bar showing where it falls.
  4. The healthy range is the weight span for a BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 at your height.

How It's Calculated

BMI is weight divided by height squared. The imperial version multiplies by 703 to convert the units.

Metric: BMI = kg ÷ m²
Imperial: BMI = (lb × 703) ÷ in²

Worked example: 180 lb at 5′10″ (70 inches) gives (180 × 703) ÷ 4,900 = 25.8 — just into the overweight band.

Because height is squared, BMI systematically reads high for tall people and low for short people. It was designed by a statistician in the 1830s to describe populations, not individuals, and it has been doing a job it was never built for ever since.

What it genuinely is useful for: tracking a trend in yourself over time, and as one input among several in a clinical screen.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are BMI's limitations?

It uses only height and weight, so it cannot distinguish muscle from fat, or where fat is carried. Visceral fat around the organs carries far more health risk than the same weight on hips and thighs, and BMI is blind to the difference. It also reads high for tall people and low for short people, because height is squared rather than cubed.

Is BMI accurate for athletes?

Often not. A muscular athlete can land in the overweight or obese range while carrying very little fat, because muscle is denser than fat. If you lift seriously or play a strength sport, body fat percentage or waist-to-height ratio will tell you far more than BMI will.

What is a better measure?

Waist-to-height ratio is the strongest single alternative and needs only a tape measure — under 0.5 is the target. Body fat percentage is more informative again but harder to measure accurately at home. In practice, no single number captures health, and a trend over months tells you more than any one reading.

Does BMI work for children?

Not in this form. Children and teenagers are assessed on age-and-sex percentile charts, because body composition changes substantially through growth. A paediatric BMI-for-age percentile is the right tool, and it should be interpreted by a clinician rather than read off a chart.
