How to Use the Gravel Calculator

  1. Measure the length and width of the area in feet. Split irregular shapes into rectangles and run each one separately.
  2. Tap the depth that matches the job — path, driveway, heavy use or base layer.
  3. Pick the gravel type. This only affects the tonnage: different stone weighs different amounts per cubic yard.
  4. Quote your supplier in whichever unit they price by. Both numbers describe the same pile.

How It's Calculated

Volume is area times depth, converted to yards. Depth is in inches, so it is divided by 12 to get feet, and the cubic feet are divided by 27 because a cubic yard is 3 × 3 × 3 feet.

Cubic yards = (length × width × (depth ÷ 12)) ÷ 27
Tons = cubic yards × density

Worked example: a 20 ft × 10 ft driveway at 3 inches is 200 sq ft × 0.25 ft = 50 cubic feet, and 50 ÷ 27 = 1.85 yd³. In crushed stone at 1.4 tons per cubic yard, that is 2.59 tons.

Densities are loose, dry weights and are approximate — 1.2 to 1.5 tons per cubic yard covers most gravels. Wet material weighs more, and a supplier's own figure for their stone beats any general number, so ask if the order is large.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should I order by the cubic yard or by the ton?

Whichever your supplier prices by — both describe the same pile. Quarries and bulk haulers usually sell by the ton, because the truck crosses a scale. Landscape yards more often sell by the cubic yard, because they load with a bucket of known volume. A cubic yard runs roughly 1.2–1.5 tons depending on the stone, so convert before comparing two quotes; the cheaper-looking unit is often just the smaller one.

How deep should gravel be?

2" is plenty for a garden path or decorative bed carrying foot traffic. 3" suits a residential driveway surface. 4" is better where trucks, trailers or regular turning are involved. 6" is a compacted base course under pavers, a shed or a new driveway. Note that a driveway built from bare soil is normally three layers totalling 8–12" — a large angular base, a middle layer, then the surface stone — rather than one deep pour.

Should I add extra for compaction?

Yes, if the gravel is being compacted. Angular crushed stone and road base lose roughly 10–20% of their loose volume once plated or rolled, so order about 15% over the calculated figure when the finished depth matters. Rounded stone — pea gravel, river rock — isn't compacted and needs no allowance beyond a little extra for spreading and low spots.
