How to Use the Pace Calculator

  1. Choose what to solve for — pace, time or distance.
  2. Enter the two values you know; time is split into hours, minutes and seconds.
  3. Pick miles or kilometres.
  4. The detail row projects finish times at that pace for common race distances.

How It's Calculated

All three modes rearrange one relationship.

Pace = time ÷ distance
Time = pace × distance
Distance = time ÷ pace

Worked example: a 5K (3.11 miles) in 25:00 is 1,500 seconds ÷ 3.11 = 482 seconds per mile, or 8:02 per mile. In kilometres that is 4:59 per km.

Race projections in the detail row assume you hold the same pace, which gets less realistic as distance grows. A pace you can hold for a 5K is not a pace you can hold for a marathon — most runners lose 30 to 60 seconds per mile between those two distances.

The useful comparison: 5:00 per kilometre and 8:03 per mile are the same speed. Runners who train in one unit and race in the other should know both.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I improve my pace?

Mostly by running more, at an easy effort, consistently. The common error is running every session at a moderate-hard pace, which is too hard to recover from and too easy to drive adaptation. Add one faster session a week to a base of easy running, and give changes months rather than weeks to show up.

What is race pace versus training pace?

Most training should be considerably slower than race pace — conversational, where you could hold a conversation. Race pace appears in specific workouts and on race day. Runners who train at race pace constantly tend to plateau and pick up injuries.

Why is my treadmill pace different from outdoors?

Treadmills remove wind resistance and the belt assists slightly, so the same effort produces a faster displayed pace. A 1% incline is the usual adjustment to approximate outdoor effort. Treadmill calibration also drifts, so the displayed number may simply be wrong.
