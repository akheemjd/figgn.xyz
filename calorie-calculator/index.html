How to Use the Calorie Burn Calculator

  1. Enter your bodyweight — it scales the whole calculation.
  2. Pick the activity; each one carries a MET value, a multiple of resting burn.
  3. Enter the duration in minutes of actual activity, not total time at the gym.
  4. The above resting figure is the honest one, since you would burn calories regardless.

How It's Calculated

MET stands for metabolic equivalent of task. One MET is roughly what you burn sitting quietly, so a 10-MET activity burns about ten times that.

Calories = MET × weight(kg) × hours

Worked example: running at 6 mph is 9.8 METs. For an 82 kg person over 45 minutes: 9.8 × 82 × 0.75 = 603 calories.

The gross figure overstates the benefit. You would have burned roughly 1 MET's worth sitting on the sofa for those 45 minutes — about 62 calories here — so the genuinely additional burn is nearer 540. That distinction is why "I earned this" arithmetic tends to go wrong.

MET values are population averages measured in labs. A trained runner is more efficient and burns slightly less at the same speed than an untrained one, and intensity within an activity varies enormously. Fitness trackers use the same rough approach and are not meaningfully more accurate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How accurate are calorie burn estimates?

Roughly, and they tend to run high. MET values are lab averages that ignore fitness, efficiency, terrain and how hard you were actually working. Errors of 20 to 30% in either direction are normal, and wearables are not meaningfully better despite the confident numbers they display.

Should I eat back the calories I burn?

Partially, if at all. Because burn estimates run high and food logging runs low, eating back the full figure is the most common way a calorie deficit quietly disappears. If you are training hard and hungry, eating back roughly half is a reasonable compromise, and letting the weight trend over a few weeks decide is better still.

Why does bodyweight change the calorie burn?

Because moving a heavier body costs more energy. The MET formula scales directly with mass, so a 100 kg person burns about a quarter more than an 80 kg person doing the identical workout. It also means burn falls as you lose weight, which is one reason progress slows over time.
